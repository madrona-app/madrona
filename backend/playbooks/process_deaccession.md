# Process a Deaccession

Use this when the institution has decided to remove an object from the permanent collection. Deaccessioning is one of the most regulated and scrutinised workflows in museum practice — it has legal, ethical, and sometimes criminal implications if done wrong. This playbook assumes the decision to deaccession has already been discussed; it covers the workflow of recording and executing that decision.

## Where to go in Madrona

**Collections → Transactions → Deaccessions** and press **New deaccession**. You can also start one from an object's workspace via the **Procedures** tab.

## Before you start

Deaccessioning is governed by your institution's collections management policy (CMP), your ethical code, and — depending on jurisdiction — law. You cannot deaccession:

- An object you don't have clear title to
- An object with outstanding donor restrictions
- An object subject to NAGPRA claim, cultural patrimony law, or similar statutes
- Tax-deductible gifts within the IRS's time window (US) or equivalent jurisdictional rules

If you're unsure about any of the above, pause and consult counsel. Madrona will flag several of these automatically based on the object's classification and acquisition history, but it can't catch everything.

## The procedure phases

Madrona's deaccession workflow has three phases:

1. **Proposal** — the case for deaccessioning, documented.
2. **Approved** — committee / board sign-off.
3. **Disposed** — the object has actually left the collection.

## Phase 1: proposal

- **Object** — the object being deaccessioned.
- **Reason** — why. Typical reasons: duplicate, outside scope, permanently damaged beyond repair, repatriation, de-accession and return. Be precise; vague reasons raise flags.
- **Proposed by** — the curator or collections staff member recommending deaccession.
- **Proposal date** — when.
- **Supporting documentation** — condition reports, provenance research, curatorial memos, legal opinions. Attach everything. A deaccession with a thin paper trail is a future problem.

## Phase 2: approved

Most institutions require committee-level approval. Madrona tracks:

- **Committee decision date**
- **Approving body** — the specific committee or officer
- **Approval conditions** — any constraints on the disposal method or destination
- **Public notice** — where required, record the date and location of any public notice of deaccession

For high-value, historically significant, or culturally sensitive items, escalation to the board of trustees is typically required. Don't advance this phase without the signed minutes.

## Phase 3: disposed

Disposal methods, in order of ethical preference (varies by code):

1. **Transfer to another public institution** — the object stays in the public trust
2. **Sale at public auction** — transparent, recorded, usually with proceeds restricted to further acquisitions per professional ethics codes
3. **Return to source community / repatriation** — for culturally affiliated material
4. **Destruction** — only for hazardous or irretrievably damaged objects, with documentation

Record:

- **Disposal date**
- **Disposal method**
- **Recipient** — the receiving institution, auction house, or community
- **Proceeds use** — if sold, what the funds will be used for (often required by your ethics code)
- **Final documentation** — signed transfer receipts, auction results, or destruction certificates

## After disposal

The object's status becomes **deaccessioned**. Madrona keeps the full record (never delete it) because:

- Future provenance researchers need to trace where the object went
- Insurance and tax authorities may audit the disposal
- Ethical reviews may revisit past deaccessions

Never delete a deaccessioned record, even years later.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting a deaccession for something the institution doesn't actually own outright
- Disposing of the object before committee approval is on paper — it happens, and it's always bad
- Using sale proceeds for operating expenses in jurisdictions or ethics codes that prohibit this
- Not notifying the original donor (where required or courteous) before disposal
- Incomplete documentation — the weak paper trail is the #1 cause of deaccessions being reopened years later
