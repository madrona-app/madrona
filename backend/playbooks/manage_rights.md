# Manage Rights on an Object

Use this when you need to document, check, or clear the intellectual property rights on a collection object — for reproduction, publication, exhibition, digital access, or any other use. Rights management is the bridge between "we own the object" and "we can use images of the object."

## Where to go in Madrona

**Collections → Rights & Reproduction → Rights** and press **New right**. You can also add a right from an object's workspace via the **Rights** section.

## What to record

1. **Object** — the object whose rights you're documenting.
2. **Rights type** — what kind of right: copyright, reproduction, publication, display, digital distribution, educational use, etc.
3. **Rights holder** — who holds the right. This may be the artist, the artist's estate, a collecting society, the museum itself, or "public domain."
4. **Status** — current clearance state: cleared, restricted, unknown, public domain, orphan work, pending research.
5. **Start and end dates** — when the right applies. Copyright has expiry dates; reproduction licenses may be time-limited.
6. **Restrictions** — any conditions on use: "no commercial reproduction", "credit required", "estate approval needed for any use", "no cropping."
7. **Notes** — provenance of the rights determination. Who researched it, what sources were consulted, what remains uncertain.

## Procedure context

The Rights Management procedure requires that you understand and document the rights status of objects before any use that depends on those rights. This includes:

- Publishing an image in a catalog or online
- Licensing a reproduction to a third party
- Displaying the object in a way that implies endorsement
- Making the object discoverable on your public website
- Allowing photography by visitors

The standard emphasises that rights status must be actively maintained — it can change when rights holders die, estates transfer, copyright terms expire, or new restrictions are imposed.

## Common rights scenarios

**Copyright still active** — the artist is alive or died fewer than 70 years ago (in most jurisdictions). You can display the object but reproduction requires the rights holder's permission. Record the holder, their contact, and any standing agreements.

**Public domain** — copyright has expired or never applied (government work, pre-copyright, etc.). Record this explicitly so future staff don't re-research it. Note the basis for the determination.

**Orphan work** — you can't identify or locate the rights holder despite reasonable effort. Document the research you did. Some jurisdictions have specific orphan work provisions.

**Museum holds copyright** — the artist assigned rights to the museum (common in commissioned works or as a condition of donation). Document the assignment.

**Creative Commons or open access** — the museum has chosen to release the image under an open license. Record which license and when the decision was made.

## Clearing rights for a specific use

When someone requests to use an image (a reproduction request, a publication, an exhibition catalog):

1. Check the object's rights records — is the rights status known?
2. If cleared or public domain, proceed per the license terms
3. If restricted, check the restrictions against the requested use
4. If unknown, flag for research before approving
5. Document the clearance decision on the use request or reproduction request record

## Common pitfalls

- Assuming "we own the object" means "we own the copyright." Ownership of the physical object and ownership of the intellectual property are separate.
- Not recording public domain determinations. The next person who asks will research it again from scratch.
- Treating rights as static. An artist who was alive when you last checked may have died, changing the copyright timeline.
- Approving reproductions without checking restrictions. "No commercial use" in the rights record means the catalog publisher can't use it without separate clearance.
